The Schrödinger Equation & Quantum Confinement

To understand charge transport in nanoscale solid-state devices, classical mechanics eventually fails us. We must abandon the idea of an electron as a distinct macroscopic billiard ball and adopt a quantum mechanical treatment. Here, an electron is described by a wave function, \(\psi(x)\).

The wave function itself is a complex mathematical tool, but its absolute square, \(|\psi(x)|^2\), gives us the probability density—the statistical likelihood of finding the electron at a specific position \(x\). To find measurable physical quantities (like energy or momentum), we apply mathematical operators to this wave function (e.g., the momentum operator \(\hat{p} = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\)).

For \(\psi(x)\) to represent a real, physical particle, it must follow strict rules:

  • Continuity: The wave function and its first derivative must be continuous everywhere. (Nature does not allow instantaneous teleportation or sharp, jagged breaks in probability).
  • Square Integrability: The total probability of finding the particle somewhere in the universe must equal exactly 1 (100%).

Phase 1: The Free Electron (No Confinement)

Imagine an electron drifting alone in a vacuum, completely unconstrained by any atomic forces or barriers. The potential energy is zero everywhere (\(V = 0\)). The 1-D time-independent Schrödinger equation is:

\[ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m} \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = E\psi \]

By rearranging the terms and defining a wave vector \(k = \sqrt{\frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}}\), we get a classic second-order differential equation:

\[ \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + k^2\psi = 0 \]

The general solution to this equation is a continuous, oscillating wave:

\[ \psi(x) = A\sin(kx) + B\cos(kx) \]

The Crucial Insight: Because there are no boundaries to constrain the wave, the wave vector \(k\) can be any real number. Consequently, the energy \(E = \frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m}\) can take on any positive value. The energy spectrum of a free electron is completely continuous.

Phase 2: The Infinite Potential Well (Particle in a Box)

Now, let's build a microscopic cage. We confine the electron to a 1-D region between \(x = 0\) and \(x = a\). Outside this region, we raise the potential energy to absolute infinity. This is the simplest model for a quantum dot or a deeply scaled transistor channel.

\[ V(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & \text{if } 0 \le x \le a \\ \infty, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Applying Boundary Conditions

Because it would require infinite energy for the electron to exist outside the well, the probability of finding it there is zero. Therefore, \(\psi(x) = 0\) for \(x < 0\) and \(x > a\).

To satisfy the rule of continuity, the wave function inside the well must smoothly drop to zero precisely at the walls:

\[ \psi(0) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \psi(a) = 0 \]

Let's apply the first boundary condition (\(x=0\)) to our general solution:

\[ \psi(0) = A\sin(0) + B\cos(0) = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad B(1) = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad B = 0 \]

This eliminates the cosine term. Our wave function simplifies to \(\psi(x) = A\sin(kx)\). Now, let's apply the second boundary condition (\(x=a\)):

\[ \psi(a) = A\sin(ka) = 0 \]

We cannot let \(A = 0\), because then \(\psi(x) = 0\) everywhere, meaning the particle doesn't exist. Therefore, the sine term itself must be zero. The sine function equals zero only at integer multiples of \(\pi\):

\[ ka = n\pi \quad \Rightarrow \quad k_n = \frac{n\pi}{a}, \quad \text{where } n = 1, 2, 3, \dots \]

(Note: We exclude \(n=0\) because it results in \(\psi=0\), the trivial "no particle" solution. We exclude negative integers because they merely flip the sign of the wave function, which doesn't change the physical probability density.)

The Consequence: Energy Quantization

By forcing the wave to "fit" perfectly inside the box, we restricted the allowed values of \(k\). Substituting \(k_n\) back into our energy equation yields:

\[ E_n = \frac{\hbar^2 k_n^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2} \]

This is the birth of quantum mechanics: Confinement directly forces discretization. The electron can no longer possess any arbitrary energy; it can only exist in specific, discrete energy levels dictated by the quantum number \(n\).

Furthermore, the lowest possible energy state (the ground state, \(n=1\)) is \(E_1 = \frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\). This is greater than zero. Unlike a classical marble which can sit perfectly still at the bottom of a bowl with zero kinetic energy, a confined quantum particle is always in motion. This is known as zero-point energy.

Normalizing the Wave Function

Finally, we must satisfy the square integrability rule. We know the electron is definitively somewhere inside the well, so the sum of all probabilities from \(0\) to \(a\) must equal 1:

\[ \int_{0}^{a} |\psi(x)|^2 dx = 1 \] \[ \int_{0}^{a} |A|^2 \sin^2\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right) dx = 1 \]

Using the half-angle trigonometric identity, the integral evaluates to \(\frac{a}{2}\). Therefore:

\[ |A|^2 \left(\frac{a}{2}\right) = 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad A = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}} \]

This gives us our final, complete, and normalized wave function for an electron in an infinite potential well:

\[ \psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}} \sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right) \]
